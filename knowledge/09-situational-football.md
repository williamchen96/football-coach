# Situational football

Games are decided in a small number of situations. Coaches practice each one separately and carry a section of the call sheet for it. Rule details here are the NFL's and change often: confirm them for the current season.

## Down and distance

- **First and 10**: the most balanced down. The goal is second and 6 or better. Play-action is at its best here because the defense must honor the run.
- **Second and short**: a free shot. A deep play-action pass costs little, since third and short is still manageable.
- **Second and long**: get half of it back. Screens, draws and quick game.
- **Third down** is planned by distance:

| Category | Distance | Offense | Defense |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short | 1-2 | Sneak, inside runs, quick outs, pick routes, play-action off the short-yardage run | Heavy front, tight man, gaps filled |
| Medium | 3-6 | Man-beaters, option routes, mesh, stick, rubs from bunch. The full playbook is open | The pressure down: simulated pressures, mugged fronts, man coverage |
| Long | 7-10 | Dagger, crossers, levels, screens and draws against soft looks | Sub rush packages, zone with eyes on the quarterback, rallying to tackle short of the sticks |
| Extra long | 11+ | Screen, draw, or a route just past the sticks; avoid the disaster | Soft zone, three- or four-man rush, keep everything in front |

- **Fourth down**: see the decision section below.

## Field zones

| Zone | Where | Priorities |
|---|---|---|
| Backed up | Own goal line to own 10 | Ball security, room for the punter. Safe runs, quick throws, no long drops in the end zone |
| Open field | Own 10 to the opponent's 35 or so | The whole call sheet |
| Fringe | About the opponent's 35 to 25 | Field-goal range is at stake: no sacks, no penalties. Four-down thinking begins |
| High red zone | 20 to 11 | The last place for vertical concepts. Seams, posts, crossers |
| Low red zone | 10 to 3 | The field is short, so vertical stretches die. Horizontal stretches, rubs, back-line routes, the quarterback's legs |
| Goal line | 2 and in | Heavy personnel, sneak, power, play-action to a tight end or fullback, fades and pick routes |

Why the red zone is different: the end line acts as an extra deep defender, safeties can sit flat-footed, and windows are tighter. Favored concepts are slant-flat, snag, smash with the corner to the back pylon, mesh and other rubs, sprint-out floods, fades and back-shoulder throws, tight-end seams and stick-nod, shovel and jet-motion plays, and quarterback runs. Defenses play more man and more Cover 0 here, and "red-zone zone" with defenders dropping to the goal line.

## Two-minute offense

- Goal: score before the half or the end of the game while managing the clock.
- Know the need (field goal or touchdown), the timeouts, and the yard line that defines field-goal range for your kicker.
- Use the sideline when there are no timeouts; use the middle when there are, since it is softer.
- The clock stops on an incomplete pass, a runner out of bounds, a spike, a timeout and a penalty. In the NFL a runner going out of bounds stops the clock until the snap only in the last 2 minutes of the first half and the last 5 minutes of the second half; otherwise it restarts when the ball is spotted.
- A spike costs a down. With enough time, a called play at the line is better.
- A sack or a tackle in bounds without a timeout is the drive's worst outcome short of a turnover.
- Offensive fouls and injuries in the final minutes can carry a 10-second runoff.
- Defenses play sideline-protecting zones, keep the ball in bounds, and tackle. A "prevent" look trades yards for time.

## Four-minute offense (protecting a lead)

- Goal: end the game with the ball. First downs matter more than points.
- Run the ball, stay in bounds, use all of the play clock, and never stop the clock voluntarily.
- A first down that lets the offense kneel out the clock ends the game, so a ball carrier with the game-ending first down should slide in bounds short of the goal line.
- **Kneel-down math**: each kneel uses about 40 seconds plus the play. If the opponent has no timeouts, three kneels run off roughly 2 minutes. Each timeout the opponent holds saves them about 40 seconds.

## Timeouts and the middle eight

- Each team has three timeouts per half. The two-minute warning is a free stoppage.
- On defense when trailing late, use timeouts before the two-minute warning to save the most clock.
- The **middle eight**, the last four minutes of the first half and the first four of the second, is a swing period. A team that scores before the half and receives the second-half kickoff can score twice without the opponent's offense touching the ball. Coaches who win the toss usually defer for this reason.

## Fourth-down decisions

Analytics has moved the league toward going for it. The general shape of the modern chart:

- **Fourth and 1 or 2**: go nearly everywhere outside your own deep territory.
- **Fourth and 3 or 4**: go from around midfield to the fringe of field-goal range, where a punt gains little and a long field goal is uncertain.
- **Inside the 5**: go for the touchdown on short distances; a failure still pins the opponent.
- Go more often when trailing, when the opponent's offense is strong, and when your own short-yardage offense is good. Kick more often when a field goal changes the number of scores needed late.

The reasoning: a punt gives up possession for about 40 yards of field position; a conversion keeps a drive worth several points on average.

## Two-point decisions

- The extra point is a 33-yard kick and is made a little less than 95 percent of the time. Two-point tries succeed a little under half the time, so the two options are close in expected value and the score decides.
- Late in games, common go-for-two situations: after a touchdown that leaves you trailing by 2, 5 or 10, or leading by 1, 4 or 5.
- **Down 14, score a touchdown**: analytics favors going for two when down 8. Making it means a touchdown and an extra point wins; missing it still leaves a touchdown and two-point try to tie.
- Early in the game, kick unless there is a matchup or a defensive penalty to exploit.

## End of half and end of game

- **Hail Mary**: three or four receivers to one spot in the end zone with a tipper and trailers. The defense keeps everyone in front, bats the ball down and boxes out.
- **Hook and lateral** and other desperation plays: defenses assign trailers to the pitch.
- **Victory formation**: a kneel-down with a safety player behind the quarterback.
- **Intentional safety**: trading two points for field position and clock when leading late.
- **Taking a knee short of the goal line**: when a score would give the opponent the ball back with time and a first down ends the game.
- **Letting the opponent score**: a rare call when the opponent can otherwise run the clock down and kick to win.
- **Icing the kicker**: a timeout just before the snap. The evidence that it works is thin.

## Overtime

- Regular season: one 10-minute period. Since 2025 both teams are guaranteed a possession, as in the playoffs. If the score is tied when the period ends, the game is a tie.
- Postseason: 15-minute periods until there is a winner, with both teams guaranteed a possession.
- With both teams guaranteed the ball, the choice between receiving first and second is now a real decision: the second team knows what it needs.

## Replay and challenges

- Each coach has two challenges and earns a third if at least one of the first two succeeds. A failed challenge costs a timeout.
- Scoring plays, turnovers, and plays after the two-minute warning and in overtime are reviewed by the booth.
- Good challenges change possession, a first down on third or fourth down, or a score.

## Situational habits of good teams

- Every player knows the down, the distance, the clock and the timeouts before the snap.
- The quarterback throws the ball away on third down in field-goal range.
- Ball carriers get down in bounds with a lead and get out of bounds when trailing.
- Defenders with a lead late tackle in bounds and do not strip at the cost of a missed tackle.
- No fouls after the play. Fifteen yards changes every one of these situations.
