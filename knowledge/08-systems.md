# Systems

A system is a philosophy plus a language. Almost every NFL staff descends from a few trees, and most modern playbooks borrow from all of them. Knowing the trees explains why teams line up and call plays the way they do.

## Offensive families

### West Coast (Bill Walsh)

- **Idea**: the short, timed pass as an extension of the run game. Horizontal stretches, receivers catching on the move, the quarterback's feet tied to each route's break.
- **Staples**: slant-flat, drive, stick, spacing, Texas, levels, bootlegs, a scripted opening sequence.
- **Language**: descriptive and long. Formation, motion, protection, then named routes for individual receivers. A famous example is "Green Right Slot, Spider 2 Y Banana": formation, a play-action protection, and the tight end's route.
- **Descendants**: Mike Holmgren, Andy Reid, Jon Gruden, Mike Shanahan, and through them most of the current league.

### Air Coryell (Don Coryell)

- **Idea**: attack vertically, with deep and intermediate timing routes and a power run game to hold the safeties.
- **Language**: routes are digits from the route tree, read left to right for the receivers, so "989" is go, post, go. Backs and tight ends get named tags. Compact and easy to change.
- **Descendants**: Joe Gibbs, Norv Turner, Mike Martz ("The Greatest Show on Turf"), Ernie Zampese.

### Erhardt-Perkins (Ron Erhardt and Ray Perkins)

- **Idea**: "pass to score, run to win", built for flexibility. A play is a pair of concepts, one for each side of the formation, each with a one-word name. Any receiver can run any spot in the concept, so the same play runs from any formation and personnel group.
- **Language**: concept words, for example "Ghost" (a stick-type concept: outside vertical, an out at 8 yards, a flat) and "Tosser" (double slants). The call is formation plus the two words.
- **Why it matters**: it makes no-huddle and formation variety cheap, and it asks more of receivers, who must read coverage and adjust. It was the base of New England's offenses under Bill Belichick.

### Shanahan and McVay (wide zone and play-action)

- **Idea**: one run, outside zone, repeated from many formations and motions, with every pass built to look like it. Condensed formations, heavy motion at the snap, and bootlegs and deep crossers behind the flowing linebackers.
- **Staples**: wide zone both ways, split-flow and toss variants, the keeper (bootleg flood), play-action dig and cross, Yankee, leak, screens off the same action.
- **What it asks**: athletic linemen who can reach and cut off, receivers who block, a quarterback who throws on time off play-action.
- **Descendants**: Kyle Shanahan, Sean McVay, Matt LaFleur, Mike McDaniel, Kevin O'Connell, Bobby Slowik and many others.

### Air Raid (Hal Mumme and Mike Leach)

- **Idea**: few plays, practiced to perfection, from spread sets with wide line splits. The quarterback gets a simple read and many repetitions.
- **Staples**: mesh, Y-Cross, four verticals, shallow, Y-Stick, Y-Corner, all curls, quick screens.
- **NFL influence**: the concepts are everywhere, usually inside a fuller offense.

### Spread and RPO offense

- **Idea**: use the width of the field and the quarterback's legs or arm to make the box count wrong for the defense. Zone read, RPOs, quick screens and tempo.
- **NFL form**: Andy Reid's Kansas City offense blended West Coast structure with spread and RPO ideas; most teams carry an RPO package.

### Heavy quarterback-run offense

- **Idea**: a running quarterback restores the offense's extra blocker, so the full gap-scheme playbook (power, counter, trap) comes back with reads attached.
- **Example**: the Baltimore offenses built around Lamar Jackson, with multiple tight ends and pullers.

### Run and Shoot

Four receivers who all adjust their routes to coverage after the snap (choice, switch, go). Its option-route thinking survives in every modern slot receiver's playbook.

## Defensive families

### Fangio and Staley (two-high shells)

- **Idea**: show two deep safeties on every snap and rotate late, so the quarterback cannot read coverage before the snap. Take away explosive passes, make offenses drive patiently, and defend the run with a light box and five-man fronts.
- **Staples**: Quarters, Cover 6, late rotations to Cover 3 and Cover 1, the Penny and 5-1 fronts.
- **How offenses answer**: run the ball at the light box, throw underneath and take profits, use play-action and motion to force the safeties to declare.

### Seattle Cover 3 (Pete Carroll)

- **Idea**: a simple single-high defense played fast. A 4-3 Under front, press-bail corners in deep thirds, a rangy free safety, a strong safety in the box.
- **How offenses answer**: flood the seams and flats with four verticals and curl-flat, attack the hook defenders with crossers, use bunch and stack to free receivers from press.

### Tampa 2 (Tony Dungy and Monte Kiffin)

- **Idea**: speed over size, a four-man rush led by a penetrating 3-technique, and Cover 2 with the Mike running the middle. Keep the ball in front and rally.
- **How offenses answer**: run at the light front, throw the hole shot and the deep middle before the Mike gets there, check down and make them tackle.

### Belichick and Saban (multiple, match-based)

- **Idea**: no fixed identity. Game-plan to remove what the opponent does best, with pattern-matching coverages that play like man after the routes declare, and fronts chosen for the opponent's run game.
- **Staples**: Cover 1 with doubles on the best receiver, match Cover 3 (Rip/Liz), match Quarters (the Cover 7 family), Tite and Mint fronts, two-gapping linemen.

### 46 (Buddy Ryan)

Eight in the box with the center and both guards covered, and pressure on nearly every down. Dominant against the offenses of its time; modern spread sets and quick passing make it a situational package.

### Zone-blitz 3-4 (Dick LeBeau and Dom Capers)

A two-gap 3-4 with fire zones: five rush from unexpected places while linemen drop into the throwing lanes.

### One-gap 3-4 (Wade Phillips)

3-4 personnel playing attacking one-gap football, with the outside linebackers as the rushers and simple coverage behind.

### Simulated-pressure defenses (Baltimore tree)

Rex Ryan, Mike Pettine, Wink Martindale and Mike Macdonald. Crowded, shifting pre-snap looks ("amoeba"), mugged A gaps, and four-man rushes from changing people. The goal is to break protection rules without giving up coverage.

### Pressure-first callers

Steve Spagnuolo's defenses bring defensive backs from depth and disguise late; Brian Flores's show Cover 0 on many downs and either bring it or bail out of it. Both rely on the quarterback being wrong about who is coming.

## How a play call is built

Whatever the language, a call carries the same pieces in roughly this order:

1. **Personnel** (sent with the substitution)
2. **Formation** and strength, plus any **shift or motion**
3. **Protection** or **run scheme**
4. **Concept** or routes, with **tags** that change one player's job
5. **Snap count**, and sometimes an **alert** or a second play to check to

Defensive calls carry: **personnel**, **front**, any **stunt or pressure**, and **coverage**, with checks that change automatically against certain formations.

## What to take from this

- Schemes are answers to other schemes. Tampa 2 answered the West Coast short pass; spread and four verticals answered Cover 2 and Cover 3; two-high match defenses answered the spread; the wide zone and heavy personnel are the current answer to light two-high boxes.
- No system wins on design. Each asks for particular players, and the best coaches fit the system to the roster.
