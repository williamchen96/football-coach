# Pressures

A pressure changes who rushes. Its goal is a free rusher or a favorable one-on-one, reached by overloading a protection, beating its rules, or making the quarterback think it is coming when it is not.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Dog | A linebacker rushes |
| Blitz | Strictly, a defensive back rushes; commonly, any extra rusher |
| Four-man rush | The standard rush, from the four linemen |
| Five-man pressure | One extra rusher, usually with zone or Cover 1 behind it |
| Six-man pressure | Two extra rushers, with Cover 0 or three-deep, two-under behind it |
| Overload | More rushers to one side than the protection has blockers there |
| Cross dog | Two linebackers cross in the A gaps |
| Mug (sugar) | Linebackers walk up into the A gaps before the snap to force one-on-one blocks and threaten the center |
| Green dog | A man-coverage defender rushes when his man stays in to block |
| Add-on | Same idea: a defender adds to the rush when his coverage responsibility blocks |
| Peel | A rusher who sees a back release to his side leaves the rush and covers him |
| Cat | Corner blitz |
| Nickel (slot) pressure | The slot defender rushes off the edge, with a safety rotating down to cover the slot |
| Bluff (show) | Showing pressure before the snap and dropping out |

## Zone pressure (fire zone)

Five rush; behind them three play deep thirds and three play underneath. The underneath players are two **seam-flat** defenders, who carry a vertical by #2 and then break to the flat, and one **hole** (middle hook) player.

- A lineman usually drops into coverage to replace the blitzer, so the offense's protection counts the wrong people.
- The linemen who do rush slant away from or toward the pressure to create the overload: a typical pattern is an end long-sticking inside, a tackle crossing the center's face, and two second-level players coming off the edge and through the B gap.
- Weak against: quick throws to the flat before the seam-flat player arrives, hot routes into the voided hook, four verticals (three deep against four), and screens thrown away from the pressure.

Made central to NFL defense by Dick LeBeau's 3-4.

## Simulated pressures and creepers

Only four rush, but not the expected four: a linebacker or defensive back comes and a lineman drops. The defense gets a pressure's confusion with a full seven in coverage.

- **Creeper**: the rusher is disguised and the dropper is a lineman, with an ordinary zone behind.
- **Sim pressure**: the look before the snap shows five or six potential rushers, often with both A gaps mugged, and only four come.
- They attack protection rules rather than numbers. A half-slide protection can end up with a guard blocking nobody and a back on a defensive end.
- These are the core of the Baltimore-rooted defenses (Rex Ryan, Mike Pettine, Wink Martindale, Mike Macdonald).

## Cover 0 pressure

Six or seven rush with man coverage and no safety. The defense has one more rusher than the offense has blockers.

- The modern version shows it and then varies the result: all come, or several drop out into passing lanes at the snap ("Cover 0 bluff"), so the hot throw is thrown to a defender.
- The offense's answers: a quick throw to the best matchup, a screen, maximum protection with a two-man shot, or a quarterback who can run past the first rusher.

## Man pressure (Cover 1 with five rushing)

Five rush, man coverage, a deep safety and no rat, or a rat and a green-dog rule. Produces one-on-one rush matchups across the line. The offense wins with rubs and crossers against the man coverage.

## Where pressures come from

- **Field or boundary**: boundary pressures arrive quicker because the rusher starts closer.
- **Strong or weak**: weak-side pressures target the back's side of a half-slide.
- **Interior**: A-gap pressure is the fastest path and stops the quarterback stepping up.
- **Edge with contain**: a pressure must keep the quarterback in the pocket, so one rusher on each side keeps outside leverage.

Defenses choose based on the protection they expect: bring two off the side where the back blocks, twist against the man side, or blitz the gap the center slides away from.

## Reading it as the offense

Before the snap:

- Count potential rushers against blockers. If the defense can bring one more, know the hot answer.
- Find the safeties. A rotation toward a slot or a tight end usually means someone on that side is coming.
- Look for depth and stance changes: a nickel creeping inside, a linebacker with his weight forward, a corner with eyes on the quarterback.
- A hard count makes disguised defenders show.

Answers, by type:

| Answer | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Redirect the protection | Sliding toward the overload turns a free rusher into a blocked one |
| Hot route or sight adjust | The ball goes where the blitzer came from |
| Quick game | The ball is out before any pressure arrives |
| Screen | The rushers run themselves out of the play |
| Draw and trap | Up-field rushers open interior lanes |
| Maximum protection with two-man routes | Wins when the coverage behind is man with no help |
| Empty and spread | Forces the defense to declare who is rushing |
| Quarterback run | Man coverage defenders have their backs turned |

## Principles for the defensive caller

- Pressure is for dictating: to a young quarterback, to a protection weakness, or on a down where a sack ends a drive.
- Every pressure gives something up. Know what it is and where the ball should go against it, then take that throw away with the coverage behind.
- Pair every pressure look with a non-pressure from the same picture, and every real blitz with a bluff.
- Tackle. Pressure that misses becomes an explosive play.
