# Game planning and coaching craft

Knowing plays is the start. The job is deciding which few to carry this week, when to call them, and how to teach them so that eleven players do them fast.

## The coaching week (Sunday game)

| Day | Coaches | Players |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Grade the last game, begin opponent film | Review, corrections, recovery |
| Tuesday | Build the game plan: base downs first, then third down, red zone and situations | Day off |
| Wednesday | Install first and second down | Practice base runs, play-action, base defense |
| Thursday | Install third down and pressure plan | Practice third down, blitz pickup, two-minute |
| Friday | Install red zone, goal line, short yardage, special situations | Short practice of those |
| Saturday | Finalize the call sheet, script the openers | Walk-through, meetings |
| Sunday | Call the game, adjust | Play |

## Scouting the opponent

Break the opponent's last four or more games into a table and look for tendencies by:

- **Personnel**: what they do against 11, 12, 21.
- **Down and distance**: coverage and pressure rates on first down, third and medium, third and long.
- **Formation**: their checks against trips, bunch, empty, tight splits, motion.
- **Field zone and hash**: red-zone coverages, boundary pressures.
- **Front and coverage structure**: base front, who has force, how the safeties fit, single-high or two-high rates.
- **People**: the best rusher and where he lines up, the weakest cover player, who communicates the calls, who misses tackles.

A tendency is useful only when it is strong and tied to a look the offense can create. "Seventy percent Cover 1 on third and 4 to 6 against bunch" is a call. "They like Cover 3" is not.

## Self-scouting

Run the same breakdown on your own team, because the opponent is doing it. Find where formation, personnel, motion or the back's alignment gives away run or pass, then either break the tendency or use it as bait: run the expected play's look and call its constraint.

## Building the plan

1. **Start with players.** What do your best players do well, and what matchups does the opponent offer?
2. **Pick the core.** A handful of runs and a dozen or so pass concepts that fit the opponent's structure, each from several formations. Cut everything else.
3. **Add answers.** For each core play, the constraint that punishes the defense for stopping it, and the check against the look that beats it.
4. **Plan the situations.** Third down by distance, red zone by yard line, backed up, two-minute, four-minute, two-point plays, must-have calls.
5. **Protect the quarterback.** A pressure plan: which protections against which looks, the hot answers, the screens.
6. **Keep it small enough to practice.** A play that has not been repeated in practice against the expected look should not be called.

Defensive plans mirror this: take away the opponent's best player and favorite concepts, decide how to play their top personnel groups and formations, choose the pressures that attack their protection rules, and prepare for their constraints.

## The call sheet

Organized by situation so a call is found in seconds:

- Openers (the script)
- First and second down runs and passes by personnel
- Play-action and shot plays
- Third down: short, medium, long
- Red zone: high, low, goal line, two-point
- Backed up, two-minute, four-minute
- Screens, draws and specials
- Answers to pressure, and "get-back-on-schedule" calls
- Best plays by hash and by coverage

## The opening script

Bill Walsh's practice of scripting the first 15 or so plays. Its purposes:

- Start fast with rehearsed plays.
- Show formations and motions to see how the defense adjusts, and record the answers for later.
- Set up later calls by showing the base play first.
- Remove emotion from early play-calling.

## Sequencing

Good play-calling is a conversation with the defensive caller:

- **Base, constraint, counter.** Run the base play until the defense changes something to stop it, then call the play that punishes that change.
- **Same look, different play.** Formations, motions and backfield actions that repeat make defenders slow.
- **Call it before they adjust.** Use a constraint when you expect the adjustment, such as after a successful run to the same side.
- **Attack the adjustment, not the defense.** When a safety starts cheating down, the plan becomes the throw behind him.
- **Tempo as a weapon.** Go fast after an explosive play or when the defense has the wrong personnel on the field.

## In-game adjustments

- Coaches in the booth chart the defense's actual calls by down, formation and personnel and compare them with the plan.
- Between series, the questions are: what are they doing that we did not expect, who is winning their matchup, and what did they change since the last drive?
- Halftime is short. Good staffs change two or three things, not the plan.
- Most "adjustments" are choosing a different part of the plan that was already practiced.

## Watching film

Use the all-22 (coaches') view. Watch each play at least three times:

1. **Before the snap**: personnel, formation, motion, the shell, the box count, corner depth and leverage. Predict the play.
2. **The front**: line steps tell you zone or gap; pullers tell you where; the defense's fits tell you who has force, alley and cutback.
3. **The coverage**: watch the safeties at the snap for rotation, then the underneath droppers, to name the coverage. Then find the read and whether the ball went where it should.

Useful questions: who was the conflict defender? Who was unblocked, and was that by design? Was a missed play a call problem, a technique problem or a player losing a matchup?

## The numbers coaches use

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| EPA (expected points added) | How much a play changed the expected points of the drive, given down, distance and field position |
| Success rate | Share of plays that keep the offense on schedule: commonly 40 percent of the needed yards on first down, 60 on second, all of it on third and fourth |
| Explosive plays | Runs of 10 or 12 yards or more and passes of 16 or 20 or more, depending on the staff. Explosive-play margin tracks winning closely |
| Turnover margin | Still the strongest single-game predictor |
| Third-down and red-zone rates | Drive finishing |
| Pressure rate and time to throw | Rush and protection quality, more stable than sacks |
| Pass rate over expected | How pass-heavy a team is compared with what the situation predicts |
| Yards before and after contact | Splits credit between blocking and the runner |
| Starting field position | The combined effect of special teams, defense and turnovers |

## Teaching players

- Teach the why first: the problem the play solves and each player's part in it.
- Install in layers: concept, then assignment, then technique, then adjustments.
- Use the same words every time. A term means one thing.
- Walk through it, then run it against the looks you expect, then run it against the look that beats it.
- Fewer things done fast beat many things done uncertainly. Players who are thinking are slow.
